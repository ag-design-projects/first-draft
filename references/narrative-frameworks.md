# Narrative Frameworks

Use research, audience, and the decision to choose the structure. Argument logic, narrative movement, and visual experience are separate choices; combine only what the task needs.

## Source grounding

The Snyder and Lupton principles below were checked against local copies of the books. All deck structures and checks are our adaptations, not formulas prescribed by the authors for business presentations. Consult `book-sources.md` for precise source locations and access limitations. Consulting structures are retained from the original skill and are not attributed to these books.

## Establish the premise

Before outlining, capture: audience and decision; central claim; evidence; person or group pursuing a goal; real obstacle; stakes; and the change being recommended. Write a one-sentence premise that makes the subject and consequence clear. If a claim is unsupported, weaken it or mark the gap. Do not improve the premise by altering facts.

Snyder's logline and title discussion in chapter 1 motivates this compression. For a presentation, usefulness and accuracy matter more than cinematic irony or marketability.

## Choose the argument structure

| Structure | Best fit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Pyramid Principle: answer, supporting arguments, evidence | Executive recommendation or short decision meeting | Can obscure a discovery process; show uncertainty and alternatives |
| Situation, complication, question, answer | Shared context followed by a consequential decision | Keep context brief; do not manufacture a complication |
| MECE issue tree and evidence ladder | Diagnostic research or strategy | Logical categories alone do not create an audience journey |
| Scenario comparison | Choosing among uncertain futures | Scenarios are possibilities, not predictions |
| Current state, gap, initiatives, governance, milestones | Transformation roadmap | A sequence of activities needs explicit outcomes and ownership |

Ghost decks and 80/20 prioritization support preparation. Use the available consulting skill for specialist business analysis when relevant, without treating its absence as a reason to fabricate analysis.

## Use Snyder selectively

Chapter 4, “Let's Beat It Out!”, offers fifteen screenplay beats. Use selected functions, not a mandatory fifteen-slide deck or screenplay page timings.

| Book device | Presentation adaptation | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Opening image and final image | Show the present condition and a comparable end condition | Distinguish measured improvement from a proposed future |
| Theme stated | Make the central question or thesis explicit early | It is a claim to examine, not an inevitable conclusion |
| Catalyst | Identify the finding or event that makes the decision timely | Use a real trigger; avoid artificial urgency |
| Debate | Address a credible objection, alternative, or implementation concern | Give the strongest objection fair treatment |
| Break into Two | Move from diagnosis to a chosen direction | Explain the decision with evidence |
| Fun and Games / promise of the premise | Demonstrate the proposed experience or solution | Show how it works rather than adding entertainment |
| Midpoint | Introduce evidence that changes the interpretation | A turning point is optional; never invent a revelation |
| Bad Guys Close In / All Is Lost | Examine constraints, risks, or failed attempts when documented | No invented villains or compulsory crisis |
| Break into Three / finale | Synthesize learning into a feasible recommendation and action | An unresolved question can remain unresolved |

For a short case-study deck, a possible arc is current condition → goal and friction → decisive evidence → options → chosen response → demonstration → limitations → outcome and next step. This is an adaptation. For an executive meeting, put the recommendation first and use the arc within the supporting case.

Chapter 5, “Building the Perfect Beast”, uses a board of scene cards to inspect structure. Adapt this into a storyboard card with:

- Beat purpose and action title.
- Audience understanding before and after; emotional intention where useful.
- Real question, obstacle, or tradeoff; allow “none” on context/reference slides.
- Claim, evidence identifier and source location, with assumptions labeled.
- Visual focal point, layout ID, content capacity, and transition to the next beat.

Move, merge, or remove redundant cards before rendering. Do not require every slide to reverse emotion or contain conflict: those are screenplay prescriptions, not presentation requirements.

## Use Lupton's action, emotion, and sensation lenses

**Action:** Narrative Arc and Storyboard encourage a sequence that depicts change rather than disconnected topics. Personas help identify a goal and friction; use observed people where possible and label composites. Scenario planning and design fiction can make a possible future tangible, with its speculative status visible.

**Emotion:** An emotional journey differs from the event sequence. Map intended audience responses, such as recognition → concern → understanding → confidence, without claiming those feelings were measured. Vary tempo based on the material. Put a difficult finding where it can be understood; end with a useful decision or next step. Lupton's discussion of the peak-end rule is not a guarantee that a presentation will persuade.

**Sensation:** Use gaze and Gestalt principles to establish a focal point, visual order, and clear groupings. Use affordances to clarify how an audience can act, especially in interactive decks and workshops. For static slides, an explicit owner and next step are a practical adaptation; they are not themselves a physical interface affordance. Avoid using color alone to encode meaning.

Do not turn every Lupton concept into a mandatory checklist item. Co-creation fits workshops; multisensory design may fit live experiences; either may be irrelevant to a short static deck.

## Options to offer

Offer genuinely different choices rather than changing framework labels on the same outline:

- Executive recommendation: answer-first argument, concrete current/future contrast.
- Evidence-led discovery: diagnostic structure with a genuine reframing insight, if supported.
- User experience: observed goal and friction, response, demonstration, and measured or proposed change.
- Future choice: scenarios, tradeoffs, and a clearly labeled speculative scene.

For each option include fit, tradeoff, premise, 6–12 slides unless the brief calls for another length, action titles, layout IDs, visuals, evidence gaps, and provenance. Preserve the existing storyboard selection step.

## Review before approval

Can the audience identify the recommendation and its evidence? Does each transition follow from the preceding material? Is the obstacle real? Are counterarguments represented? Does the ending deliver the premise? Can viewers distinguish results, forecasts, and illustrations? Is any beat present only to imitate a movie?

Treat layout as part of storyboarding. Split beats that exceed suitable layout capacity. Use images, quotes, charts, or a pause only when they serve the argument. Apply `storytelling-writing.md` before rendering.
